Why Did It Start?

In Lesson 01 you saw two wars start separately, in 1937 and 1939. This lesson explains why. The causes form a chain of five links, and both Germany and Japan went through the same five. Learn the chain once and you can explain both wars.

LinkGermanyJapan
1. A motiveResentment of the 1919 peace treatyAn empire short of raw materials
2. A crashThe Great Depression, from 1929Depression, worst in the countryside
3. Hardliners in chargeHitler made chancellor, 1933The army acting on its own
4. Grabs, step by step1936–39: Rhineland to Czechoslovakia1931 Manchuria, 1937 China

1. A motive

Germany lost the First World War, and the Treaty of Versailles (1919) made it pay. It lost 13 percent of its territory and 10 percent of its population. Its army was capped at 100,000 men, and Article 231, the “War Guilt Clause”, made it accept full responsibility for starting the war. Most Germans saw the treaty as a Diktat, a dictated peace. Overturning it became the rallying cry of the right-wing parties, the Nazis among them.USHMM Holocaust Encyclopedia, “Treaty of Versailles.”

Japan had won its wars and built an empire, but it lacked the raw materials a modern power runs on. It got most of its oil from the United States and its rubber from British Malaya. Many Japanese came to believe their country could, and should, control East Asia by force.Columbia University, Asia for Educators, “Japan’s Quest for Power and World War II in Asia.”

2. A crash

The Great Depression reached Germany in late 1929. Banks collapsed in 1931 and the government seemed unable to cope. Look at what happened to the Nazi vote:

Japan’s depression began earlier, in 1926. In the countryside it was devastating and poverty was widespread. Young soldiers from those villages were among the strongest supporters of expanding Japan’s territory to secure its economy.Columbia, “Japan’s Quest for Power.”

3. Hardliners in charge

Hitler did not win power at the ballot box. After the 1932 elections no party could form a majority. On 30 January 1933 President Hindenburg appointed Hitler chancellor, persuaded by conservative advisers who “felt confident that they could control Hitler.”USHMM, “The Nazi Rise to Power.” They could not.

In Japan no single institution held full control of government, and the army increasingly acted on its own, both on the Asian mainland and in politics at home.Columbia, “Japan’s Quest for Power.”

4. Grabs, step by step

Neither power went to war all at once. Each took a piece, waited to see whether anyone would respond, then took more.

Sep 1931JapanUses a railway explosion at Mukden as a pretext to invade Manchuria. It becomes the puppet state of Manchukuo, run by the local Japanese army.
Mar 1935GermanyReintroduces conscription, in open violation of Versailles
Mar 1936GermanySends troops into the demilitarized Rhineland. Britain and France protest but do nothing.
Jul 1937JapanFull-scale war with China (Lesson 01)
Mar 1938GermanyAnnexes Austria (the Anschluss)
Sep 1938GermanyGets the Sudetenland, the German-speaking border region of Czechoslovakia, at Munich
Mar 1939GermanySeizes the rest of Czechoslovakia, then demands Danzig and a route across Poland

The Japanese steps come from the US State Department’s historians, who note speculation that mid-level Japanese officers planted the Mukden bomb themselves.Office of the Historian, US Department of State, “The Mukden Incident of 1931 and the Stimson Doctrine.” The German steps come from the Holocaust Encyclopedia.USHMM Holocaust Encyclopedia, “German Prewar Expansion.”

5. Nobody stops them

In Asia, the League of Nations, the international body set up after the First World War to keep the peace, sent a commission to Manchuria. Its report refused to recognize Manchukuo. There was little support for sanctions, and when the League adopted the report in 1933, Japan’s delegation walked out and never returned.Office of the Historian, “The Mukden Incident.”

In Europe, Britain and France followed a policy of appeasement: easing tension by giving way on the other side’s grievances. At Munich in September 1938 they agreed that Czechoslovakia must hand the Sudetenland to Germany, and no Czech representative was in the room. The British prime minister, Neville Chamberlain, flew home and said, “I believe it is peace for our time.” He had his reasons: neither Britain nor France was ready for war, and British officials feared German bombers would devastate their cities.Sean Scanlon, “Appeasement and ‘Peace for Our Time,’” The National WWII Museum, 2024.

Six months later Hitler took the rest of Czechoslovakia, which was not German-speaking at all. That broke appeasement. Britain and France guaranteed Poland’s borders. When Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, with the USSR secretly on board, the guarantee meant war.

Your viewing and reading

Watch: The World at War, episode 1, “A New Germany (1933–1939)” (about 52 minutes; on DVD, and on streaming services in some countries, such as Apple TV in the UK). It covers links 2 to 5 for Germany, told by people who lived through them. As you watch, spot each link of the chain.

Read: “Japan’s Quest for Power and World War II in Asia” (Columbia University, about 15 minutes). The episode is all Germany; this covers the Asian half.

Bring one answer to our next session: was appeasement a mistake, or the best of bad options in 1938? Historians still argue about it. For both sides of the argument, read the Imperial War Museums’ “Was Chamberlain Wrong to Appease Hitler?”

Practice: retrieval

Some of these go back to Lesson 01 on purpose: returning to older material after a gap is what makes it stick.

How did the Nazi share of the vote change between 1928 and July 1932?

From 2.6% in 1928 to 18% in 1930 and 37% in July 1932. That made the Nazis the largest party, but never a majority.

In Germany, which link in the chain most explains that rise?

The Depression reached Germany in late 1929. The jump from 2.6% to 18% came in the very next election, in 1930.

How did Hitler become chancellor in January 1933?

President Hindenburg appointed him on 30 January 1933. His conservative advisers thought they could control Hitler.

In what order did Hitler’s grabs come, 1936–1939?

Rhineland (Mar 1936), Austria (Mar 1938), Sudetenland at Munich (Sep 1938), the rest of Czechoslovakia (Mar 1939). Each step tested whether anyone would respond.

Why did Chamberlain agree to the Munich deal in 1938?

Britain and France were not ready militarily, and British officials feared German bombing of their cities. Czechoslovakia wasn’t even in the room.

What did the League of Nations do about Japan seizing Manchuria?

Its report refused to recognize Manchukuo, but there was little support for sanctions. Japan walked out of the League in 1933.

Which link was Japan’s motive?

Japan depended on others for oil (mostly from the US) and rubber (from British Malaya). Japan was on the winning side in 1919, so Versailles was Germany’s grievance, not Japan’s.

Which step ended appeasement and led to the guarantee to Poland?

In March 1939 Hitler took the non-German parts of Czechoslovakia, breaking the Munich deal. After that, his claims to be uniting Germans could no longer be believed.

Which event put the USSR on the Allied side?

Operation Barbarossa, 22 June 1941. The USSR was Germany’s partner from 1939 but never joined the Axis.

In WWII usage, what does “the Eastern Front” mean?

The Eastern Front is Germany and its allies against the USSR, from June 1941 to May 1945. America’s war with Japan is the Pacific theatre.

How long after Germany’s surrender did Japan announce its own?

Germany surrendered on 8 May 1945. Japan announced its surrender in mid-August and signed on 2 September.

Practice: explain it

Without scrolling up, explain why the war started in five sentences, one per link. In each sentence, say what happened in Germany and in Japan.

Compare with a model answer

Germany resented the Treaty of Versailles, while Japan wanted the raw materials its empire lacked. The Great Depression hit both, and made voters and soldiers open to extreme answers. In Germany, Hitler was appointed chancellor in 1933, and in Japan, the army increasingly acted on its own. Japan took Manchuria in 1931 and invaded China in 1937, while Germany took the Rhineland, Austria and then Czechoslovakia, one step at a time. Nobody stopped them: the League of Nations did nothing about Manchuria, and Britain and France appeased Hitler until he invaded Poland in 1939.
